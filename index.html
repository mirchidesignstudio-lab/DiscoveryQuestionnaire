    The 3 principles we design with · tap a card for the brand vs style split and why it helps you
    a) Gestalt psychology

    The mind sees the whole before the parts.

    You read a circle, not ten dots. The brain automatically groups things that sit close together, look alike or follow one line, and fills in gaps to make a complete shape.

    Customers do the same with your brand: a post, a menu and a website are judged as one impression, not separately.

    From Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka, 1920s): proximity, similarity, continuity, closure.
    Gestalt, split
    BrandOne core promise, said the same way at every touchpoint, so separate messages add up to one clear idea.
    StyleA shared grid, spacing, colour roles and type hierarchy, so every piece is instantly seen as part of one family.
    Why it helps youPeople recognise you before they read your name. Each new post builds on the last instead of starting from zero, so the same budget gets more recall.
    Used in: your brand kit (step 4) and style setup (step 6).
    b) Jakob's Law

    People expect you to work like everything else they know.

    Your customers spend most of their time with other brands, apps and shops. So they arrive with ready-made expectations: where the menu is, what a price tag looks like, which words belong to your category.

    Meet those expectations and they relax. Break them without reason and they hesitate or leave.

    Named by usability researcher Jakob Nielsen (Nielsen Norman Group). We apply it to brands through association and familiarity.
    Association & familiarity, split
    BrandWe keep the words customers already link to your category, so you're understood at once, then attach associations only you own.
    StyleFamiliar layouts, navigation and cues so nothing has to be learned. Your colour, type and voice sit on top of that.
    Why it helps youLess effort to understand means faster trust and fewer drop-offs. Attention goes to your offer, not to working out the design.
    Used in: your brand words (step 3).
    c) Disruption hook

    The brain filters out the expected. One deliberate break gets noticed.

    In a feed full of similar posts, the eye skips what it has seen before. The one item that differs is the one that gets looked at, and remembered.

    Familiarity earns trust; a single, planned surprise earns attention. We need both.

    Built on the isolation effect (von Restorff, 1933) and the “pattern interrupt” used in advertising.
    Disruption hook, split
    BrandOne surprising truth, attitude or offer that only you can claim: the reason to remember you.
    StyleOne controlled contrast (colour, crop, scale or motion) placed exactly where the message matters.
    Why it helps youAttention is the first cost of any post or ad. A hook stops the scroll; Gestalt and Jakob's Law make sure people still know it's you.
    We bring this in at the moodboard stage, after your brief.
